# Daytime surface heat misses what mosquitoes meet

Yes, open papers confirm what *Aedes aegypti* responds to, but unevenly. The laboratory and mechanistic evidence is strong. Transmission depends on air temperature in a unimodal, asymmetric way, with an optimum near 29 °C and limits near 18 °C and 34–35 °C. Daily fluctuation around the same mean changes transmission, and low humidity shortens adult life. Field evidence on what the mosquito actually meets is thin and comes almost entirely from outside Brazil. Shaded and indoor resting sites have means similar to outdoor air but a narrower daily range. Water in containers departs from air temperature by amounts that depend on the material. In Brazilian cities most pupae come from containers people fill, not from rain. Drought raises dengue 3–5 months later, which the authors attribute to household water storage. The Rio de Janeiro regression does not use an air temperature. Its exposure is MODIS daytime land-surface temperature (`LST_Day_1km`, 8-day clear-sky composites), which is the furthest of the available proxies from what the mosquito meets. It is the skin temperature of roofs, roads and canopy. It runs warm of air, most of all over urban pixels, and it measures the daily peak that indoor buffering removes. Night-time LST tracks minimum air temperature better, and so is closer to what the field studies favour. Because the product is clear-sky only, cloudy wet-season weeks, which are peak dengue weeks, drop out of the model more often, so the sample is not random with respect to rainfall or cases. And because an 8-day composite spans week boundaries, adjacent weeks share exposure values. In the fitted model, both leads (temperature after the cases) equal the lag-0 coefficient, which is the clearest sign that the lag-0 association of 1.004 per °C of LST cannot be read as transmission. The 6–8 week coefficients (about 1.02) fall in the window the biology points to, but nothing here establishes that they are causal. CHIRPS `precip_total_semana`, the rainfall adjustment, measures rain on a ~5.5 km cell, not water in containers, and it has been validated in the state only at monthly scale. InfoDengue's `tempmed`, which Validation Lab check 37 fits, is the proxy least distorted by indoor buffering. Even so, no open study validates its week-to-week anomalies in Rio de Janeiro state.

## Laboratory curves firmly establish what temperature does to the mosquito

The best-established evidence describes the mosquito's physiology, not its exposure. In mechanistic trait-based models, relative R0 for *Ae. aegypti*-borne transmission at constant temperature peaks at **29.1 °C (credible interval 28.4–29.8)**. It falls to zero at a **lower limit of 17.8 °C (14.6–21.2)** and an **upper limit of 34.6 °C (34.1–35.6)** ([Mordecai et al. 2017, PLoS NTD, publisher page; key numbers checked, supplements not read](https://journals.plos.org/plosntds/article?id=10.1371/journal.pntd.0005568)). The two ends differ in kind. The hot end is sharp and well constrained because adult survival collapses there, even though the virus's own extrinsic incubation rate keeps rising to about 35 °C. The cold end is gradual and poorly constrained. A synthesis across 11 pathogens and 15 mosquito species found the same unimodal shape, with dengue and Zika among the highest optima ([Mordecai et al. 2019, Ecol Lett, Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC6744319)). Adult survival models fitted to 351 laboratory and 59 field experiments show that *Ae. aegypti* tolerates a wider temperature range than *Ae. albopictus*. The gap between the laboratory and field fits shows how much mortality comes from causes other than temperature ([Brady et al. 2013, Parasit Vectors, Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC3867219)). These curves come from a few colonies and virus strains. They are model optima, not observed population optima, and local adaptation was not reviewed.

**Daily fluctuation is the second firmly confirmed variable, and its direction depends on the mean.** At a mean of 26 °C, mosquitoes under a larger daily temperature range (DTR) "were less susceptible to virus infection and died faster" in two experiments with different serotypes. A thermodynamic model predicted that larger DTR raises transmission below a mean of about 18 °C and lowers it above ([Lambrechts et al. 2011, PNAS, Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC3088608)). The cold-side prediction was confirmed in direction at a 20 °C mean. A large DTR shortened extrinsic incubation from 29.6 to 18.9 days and raised disseminated infection by 60% ([Carrington et al. 2013, PLoS NTD, Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC3636080)). Adding a fixed 8 °C DTR to the Mordecai model moved the *Ae. aegypti* limits to 13.5 °C and 34.2 °C, widening the cold side and narrowing the hot side ([Mordecai et al. 2017](https://journals.plos.org/plosntds/article?id=10.1371/journal.pntd.0005568)). Epidemic potential peaked at a 29.3 °C mean with no DTR, but at 20 °C with a 20 °C DTR ([Liu-Helmersson et al. 2014, PLoS ONE, abstract via search only](https://doaj.org/article/bde2149f9142439a9837444b5b687fb9)). This is Jensen's inequality applied to a curved response. The mean of the response over a week is not the response at the weekly mean. The size of the gap depends on where the week sits on the curve.

**Humidity is confirmed as a survival variable, on a small evidence base.** A pooled survival analysis of 17 studies (192 experiments) found a "strong temperature-dependent association of humidity with mortality". Mortality rose non-linearly as humidity fell, and lowest mortality for *Ae. aegypti* was near 27.5 °C. The authors warn that "data are limited at low humidities, temperature extremes" and expect desiccation to matter most in arid regions ([Schmidt et al. 2018, Parasit Vectors, Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5918765)). Their model uses vapour pressure deficit, which combines temperature and humidity, rather than relative humidity alone. In Puerto Rico, a population model fitted surveillance traps and municipal dengue incidence better when it added both humidity-dependent survival and rain-triggered egg hatching than with either alone ([Lega, Brown and Barrera 2017, author manuscript, Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5850122)). No open study found here shows humidity acting on vector competence or extrinsic incubation directly. Recent humidity literature (2019–2026) is under-covered because one search failed.

## Resting sites read steadier than stations, but the numbers come from Georgia and Colombia

The question of what air the mosquito actually rests in has open numeric answers only from a temperate US city and from fifteen Colombian houses. In Athens, Georgia, loggers hung in shaded vegetation where *Ae. albopictus* rests showed that the weather station **overpredicted summer daily mean temperature by 1.3–1.8 °C, daily maximum by 3.0–4.2 °C and daily range by 3.1–3.7 °C**. It **underpredicted mean relative humidity by 6.8–13.3 points**. In fall the mean bias shrank to 0.3–0.7 °C. The ranking of urban, suburban and rural sites by modelled transmission potential reversed between seasons ([Murdock et al. 2017, PLoS NTD, full text read](https://journals.plos.org/plosntds/article?id=10.1371/journal.pntd.0005640)). A follow-up with 51 loggers found that **vectorial capacity computed from the station exceeded the microclimate distribution in every month**, by more than twofold in September–October. Minimum temperature rose with impervious surface, and maximum temperature fell with tree cover ([Wimberly et al. 2020, PLoS NTD, full text read; coefficient table and station-minus-logger figure not extracted](https://journals.plos.org/plosntds/article?id=10.1371/journal.pntd.0008614)). The error is therefore structured by season and land cover. It is not random noise.

For *Ae. aegypti*, which rests indoors, the Colombian evidence matters most. In Acapulco, resting adults were found in bedrooms (44%), living rooms (25%) and bathrooms (20%), mostly below 1.5 m ([Dzul-Manzanilla et al. 2016, J Med Entomol, NSF PAR open copy, abstract](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10026228-indoor-resting-behavior-aedes-aegypti-diptera-culicidae-acapulco-mexico)). In 15 households across Neiva, Sincelejo and Soledad, **indoor temperatures had a smaller daily range than outdoor ones, with relatively similar means**. The numbers sit in a supplementary table that was not reached. The choice of temperature source moved the share of weeks with R0 > 1 under the Mordecai curves from 87.8% (indoor) to 28.6% (station) in Neiva. In Sincelejo the ordering ran the other way, and under two other thermal models every source gave R0 > 1 in every week ([Peña-García et al. 2023, Microorganisms, full text via Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10223750/fullTextXML)). Laboratory colonies held under Neiva-derived indoor and outdoor profiles showed little change in mortality or competence. A simulated chikungunya epidemic, however, peaked higher and earlier under the indoor profile ([Turner et al. 2024, Pathogens, Europe PMC abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=PMCID:PMC11728849&resultType=core&format=json)).

Taken together, buffering acts mainly on the daily range and the extremes, much less on the mean. That has a direct consequence for the proxies. A weekly mean air temperature is the summary least distorted by the indoor–outdoor difference. A weekly maximum or DTR from a station or grid is the most distorted. The evidence for this comes from one Colombian study whose numbers were not read and one temperate study of another species. No open Brazilian study reports station-minus-resting-site differences in °C or relative humidity. Loggers in nine João Pessoa neighbourhoods were mounted on telecommunications antennas, not at resting sites, and their magnitudes were not reached ([UFPB repository](https://repositorio.ufpb.br/jspui/handle/123456789/19958)).

## Containers decouple larvae from air temperature and from rain

Larvae meet water temperature, and water is not air. Delhi loggers recorded monthly-mean water temperature by container material: ceramic pots 14.3–28.5 °C, underground cement tanks 23.3–30.4 °C, overhead tanks 15.8–35.1 °C, against air at 17.7–36.1 °C. Water was cooler than air in every container type. The microsite air loggers themselves read warmer than the local weather station ([Kumar et al. 2018, Geospatial Health, abstract only](https://geospatialhealth.net/gh/article/view/707)). That makes two layers of offset between a gridded air temperature and the larva. The sign is not fixed. In sun-exposed small containers in Cairns, water reached high temperatures at mild air temperatures, while tyres stayed near shaded air temperature ([Richardson et al. 2013, JCU repository record; open copy not confirmed](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/26457/)). The one Rio de Janeiro field study, 240 containers in four neighbourhoods in April–August 2010, dropped air temperature from its models because it correlated with water temperature at r > 0.7. It found immature abundance rising with water volume, and adult size rising slightly with water temperature (female wing length +0.02 mm per °C). The authors did not report an air–water offset ([David et al. 2021, Front Ecol Evol, full text read](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fevo.2021.626757)).

**Rain reaches only some of the containers that matter.** A pupal productivity survey in Goiânia found that elevated water tanks, gutters and water-holding roofs were under 40% of positive containers but over 70% of pupae. Routine LIRAa surveys "did not accurately identify or target the containers that were the most important to adult mosquito production" ([Pilger et al. 2011, Trop Med Int Health, LSHTM Research Online, abstract](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/448)). In Fortaleza, irregular supply drove frequent use of tanks, cisterns, barrels and pots. Large covered containers that keep females from the water broke that link ([Caprara et al. 2009, Cad Saúde Pública, SciELO](https://scielosp.org/article/csp/2009.v25suppl1/S125-S136/en/)). No national or multi-city estimate exists of the share of pupae from stored water versus rain-filled containers. It depends on supply reliability and housing stock, so it cannot be carried from one city to another.

Where rain does reach containers, its effect is non-linear. Weeks with five or more storm-drain flushing days in Singapore cut dengue outbreak risk by 16–70% over the next 1–6 weeks. No single rainfall variable predicted flushing ([Benedum et al. 2018, PLoS NTD, full text read](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6283346)). That evidence covers roadside drains, and *Ae. aegypti* immatures resist flushing better than *Culex*, so it probably overstates flushing in domestic containers. In Rio de Janeiro, ovitrap egg indices tracked temperature and rainfall at a one-week lag, and weekly mean temperature above 22–24 °C went with high abundance ([Honório et al. 2009, J Med Entomol, Fiocruz ARCA record, abstract](https://www.arca.fiocruz.br/handle/icict/1259)).

**Drought acts through people, months later.** Across 558 Brazilian microregions (2001–2019), extremely wet conditions raised dengue risk with a **peak RR of 1.56 (1.41–1.73) at a one-month lag**. Extreme drought raised it with a **peak RR of 1.43 (1.22–1.67) at four months**. The drought effect was **1.60 (1.33–1.92) in highly urbanised microregions against 1.15 (1.08–1.22) in rural ones**, and stronger where supply interruptions reported to SNIS were more frequent. Drought was measured with the self-calibrating Palmer index at 0.5°. The model including drought beat the baseline in 84% of Southeast microregions ([Lowe et al. 2021, Lancet Planet Health, LSHTM open PDF, full text read; appendix with the interruption interaction not read](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/4660590/1/2021_lowe_LancetPlanHealth.pdf)). The authors attribute the delay to improvised household storage. They observed neither storage nor mosquitoes, so the pathway is an interpretation. A comparable 3–5 month drought signal appears in Barbados ([Lowe et al. 2018, PLoS Med, abstract via snippets](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6049902)), in 291 Southeast Asian locations (cumulative RR 1.85, 1.73–1.99) ([Ith et al. 2025, PLoS NTD, full text read](https://journals.plos.org/plosntds/article?id=10.1371/journal.pntd.0012649)) and in Vietnam ([Gibb et al. 2023, Nat Commun, Europe PMC abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/s41467-023-43954-0&resultType=core&format=json)). Three of these four share an author and a method family, so they are not independent replications. No drought–dengue study specific to Rio de Janeiro state was found.

## Daytime LST is the exposure, and the furthest proxy from the mosquito

The analysis file is the multimodal dengue dataset by Sánchez-Marqués and Sánchez ([Zenodo, CC BY 4.0](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22029053)), built by [code at commit 5c4d30c](https://github.com/jssanchez-garreta/dataset-multimodal-dengue-brazil/tree/5c4d30cd01fb17ea44dcbd416d162c272aa92257). The regressions take their temperature exposure from neither InfoDengue nor a reanalysis. Every regression in both lag workflows uses **`LST_Day_1km`**, which Fieldwork reads from MODIS MOD11A2 files: daytime, 1 km, clear-sky land-surface temperature in 8-day composites. Fieldwork samples it over each municipality and assigns it to each week whose dates fall in the composite period. A week whose period has no clear-sky value is dropped, with the stated reason: "A week whose satellite period has no clear-sky value has no exposure to model." The 2024 workflow (W4) adjusts for month (`mes`) with municipality strata. The 2010–2025 workflow (W5), whose results are discussed below, adjusts for `precip_total_semana` with strata of municipality × year × month. That rainfall column is CHIRPS v2.0 at the cell nearest each municipality's coordinates, summed over ISO weeks, with a missing day counted as 0 (`scripts/data_construction/03_build_dataset_precipitacion_chirps_anual.py`).

**LST is skin temperature, and daytime LST is the least air-like part of it.** Validation studies find that night-time LST tracks minimum air temperature at an RMSE of about 1–2 K. Daytime LST runs warm of air and agrees least well, especially over non-forested, heterogeneous pixels, which is to say cities ([NASA NTRS, MODIS against stations, continental US](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150019667/downloads/20150019667.pdf)). A review puts it this way: "a good agreement between minimum Ta and nighttime Ts was found in several studies. However, differences between maximum Ta and daytime Ts are relatively larger" ([MDPI review page](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-0494/4/3/17)). A multi-site urban study reported an RMSE of 1.27 K at night against 2.31 K by day; its climate zone is not stated ([AVESIS record, snippet](https://avesis.comu.edu.tr/yayin/c6fa2505-779a-4b8c-9abc-a96d6505bddb/estimating-the-near-surface-air-temperature-field-using-satellite-based-remote-sensing-of-land-surface-temperatures)). The closest Brazilian figures come from the São Francisco basin, where Aqua daytime LST explained **only 46–54% of the variation in maximum air temperature** ([Preprints.org 202503.1298](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202503.1298)). One more consequence follows from that figure, as an inference: a degree of LST does not correspond to a degree of air, so a rate ratio per °C of LST is not on the same scale as rate ratios per °C of air temperature in the literature. In metropolitan Rio, seasonal urban–rural contrasts in 8-day LST reached about 25 °C ([UFRJ Anuário, snippet](https://revistas.ufrj.br/index.php/aigeo/article/view/7827)), far beyond typical air-temperature heat-island magnitudes. In Córdoba, Argentina, LST varied by up to 5 °C within one city ([IRIS UniPV, search result](https://iris.unipv.it/handle/11571/1550701)). Wimberly and co-authors state that "satellite measures of land surface temperature do not reflect the air temperatures that people experience" ([NSF PAR 10307594](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10307594-micro-climate-macro-risk-mapping-fine-scale-differences-mosquito-borne-disease-risk-using-remote-sensing)). No open study calibrates MODIS LST against air temperature in any Brazilian city. A São Paulo comparison exists, but only its title was seen ([BrCris](https://brcris.ibict.br/vivo/display/publ_258aad62-e59f-4b19-9820-73dc5b45aac1)). No study compares LST with indoor or resting-site temperature for *Aedes*.

Measured against the mosquito, daytime LST has three properties that matter. First, it captures the daytime peak of an exposed surface, while the evidence above shows indoor and shaded resting sites remove much of that peak and keep the mean. Second, where entomology has been set against both products, night LST did better: in central Mexico, *Ae. aegypti* pupal abundance correlated more strongly with night-time than with daytime MODIS LST ([Moreno-Madriñán et al. 2014, ISPRS IJGI, MDPI open access](https://mdpi.com/2220-9964/3/2/732)). This fits the field studies favouring minimum temperature (Barbados, Minas Gerais and the national Brazilian model, discussed below). Third, the clear-sky constraint selects the weeks. In Rio's cloudy wet season, which is also peak dengue season, composites without a clear-sky value are commoner, so dropped weeks cluster in rainy, high-incidence periods. The missingness is therefore not random with respect to rainfall or cases. The composite itself averages only the clear days within its eight, so even a retained week's value describes its sunniest days.

InfoDengue's `tempmed` remains relevant for two reasons. Validation Lab check 37 fits it, with `precip_total_semana`, for two municipalities over 2010–2025 with strata of municipality × year × month. And the dataset also carries `tempmin`, so an air-temperature comparison is already possible in the file. `tempmed` comes from InfoDengue's alertcity API (`scripts/data_construction/01_download_infodengue_nacional.py`). InfoDengue's temperature began as **weekly minimum temperature from four airport stations** in Rio de Janeiro city, one per group of health districts. Its alert rule fired at Tmin above 22 °C for three weeks. The authors found that cutoff "too high" for the cooler Health District 5.x and suspected "some specificity of the meteorological station of the airport" ([Codeço et al. 2016, bioRxiv, full text read](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/046193v1.full)). The current repository says weather data come "through Mosqlimate from Copernicus ERA5 reanalysis" ([AlertaDengue README, read](https://github.com/AlertaDengue/AlertaDengue)). Mosqlimate takes hourly ERA5-Land grids, weights each pixel by its overlap with the municipal polygon, averages spatially first, and then takes daily min, mean and max ([Mosqlimate API docs, read](https://api.mosqlimate.org/docs/datastore/GET/climate/); [satellite-weather-downloader README, read](https://github.com/osl-incubator/satellite-weather-downloader)). Mosqlimate's platform paper names ERA5 at 0.25° instead, and reports no validation ([Ganem et al. 2024, arXiv, read](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.18945v1)). Four things remain unknown: when the switch happened, whether 2010–2025 was reprocessed, which station served each non-capital municipality in the station era, and how weekly `tempmed` is formed from daily values. The accuracy evidence for the reanalysis is regional and favourable for the mean. Against 11 Brazilian flux towers, ERA5-Land ranked best among gridded products at daily scale for every variable except precipitation, and its daily error came mainly from imperfect correlation rather than bias. No tower was in the Atlantic Forest or Rio de Janeiro state ([Brown et al. 2025/2026, HESS preprint page, read](https://egusphere.copernicus.org/preprints/2025/egusphere-2025-883/)). In Pernambuco, mean temperature was underestimated by up to about 0.8 °C, with RMSE mostly under 0.6 °C ([Araújo et al. 2022, snippet only; paywalled, open copy not confirmed](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4YR5YyMl)). A Brazil-wide INMET comparison reportedly found ERA5-Land good for Tmin but biased for Tmax and heat extremes ([MDPI, snippet only, page blocked](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-0494/5/1/3/htm)).

CHIRPS has one open validation inside the state. Across five gauges in Niterói and São Gonçalo (2005–2018, monthly), **r was 0.71–0.87 and CHIRPS overestimated at every gauge, by 3.9% to 51.7%**. The worst case was the seafront Copacabana gauge, and the authors cite coastal proximity and orography as sources of error ([Tórnio et al. 2024, Rev Bras Climatol, full text read](https://ojs.ufgd.edu.br/rbclima/article/download/17317/9936/62569)). In Northeast Brazil CHIRPS detected rain days poorly, overestimating low rainfall and underestimating high rainfall ([Paredes-Trejo et al. 2017, core.ac.uk copy, snippet only](https://core.ac.uk/download/322446387.pdf)).

Three further inferences follow; none has been tested. A 1 km LST pixel resolves the urban lowland better than a reanalysis cell, but it resolves the wrong quantity: a hot roof says little about a shaded bedroom. A nearest-cell rainfall value, judging by the Copacabana gauge, can sit far from the rain on the populated part of a coastal municipality. And counting a missing CHIRPS day as zero biases affected weeks downward; the notes did not establish how often days are missing, so the size of this bias is unknown.

## What the Rio de Janeiro regression can and cannot say

The strata decide what the exposure has to get right. With roughly 92 × 16 × 12 strata of four to five weeks each, the W5 coefficient is identified only from **week-to-week variation in daytime LST within one calendar month in one municipality**, and only among the weeks that kept a clear-sky value. Any LST-minus-air offset that is constant within a month drops out. The question then becomes whether within-month LST anomalies track within-month changes in the air the mosquito rests in. No open study measures this anywhere in Brazil. There is a mechanistic reason for doubt, though it is an inference: from week to week, a surface's daytime skin temperature moves with cloud, sun and surface wetness as well as with air temperature. A dry, sunny composite after a rainless spell reads hot in a way the shaded air does not. Part of the within-month LST signal is therefore a rainfall and sky signal. The `precip_total_semana` adjustment absorbs some of it, but a linear term on a nearest-cell weekly total absorbs it only coarsely. If the remaining error behaves like classical error, it attenuates the coefficient toward 1. If it varies with season, land cover or cloudiness, the direction of bias is not established.

**Selection by cloud acts within the strata as well.** A month-stratum contributes only through its retained weeks. In the wet season, a stratum can lose most of its weeks, and a stratum left with one week contributes nothing. The retained weeks are the clearer, drier, sunnier ones, so the estimate describes dengue's relation to temperature in clear weeks, weighted toward drier months. Whether that relation differs from the one in cloudy weeks is not established. Neither is the share of weeks dropped by month, which the run could report directly.

**The leads are the most informative coefficients.** Temperature measured after the cases cannot cause them, yet both leads (1.005 and 1.005) equal or exceed the lag-0 estimate (1.004, 1.002–1.006). An association of the lag-0 size therefore arises without causation. The 8-day composite contributes something to this by construction. Adjacent weeks can draw on the same composite period, so short leads and lags share exposure values. Within-month seasonal slopes, autocorrelation and reporting artefacts are other candidates; which ones operate here is not established. Mechanism argues against lag 0 as well. Temperature acts through larval development, adult survival and extrinsic incubation, which together take weeks. Ovitrap indices in Rio followed temperature at one week, and the national Brazilian model found Tmin effects peaking at 2–4 months ([Lowe et al. 2021](https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/4660590/1/2021_lowe_LancetPlanHealth.pdf)). Each lag and lead is fitted in its own model, one at a time, never jointly. Each estimate therefore carries the effects of the neighbouring lags its exposure correlates with, and composite sharing strengthens that correlation. Collinearity between lags in one model cannot explain the pattern. The 6- and 8-week coefficients (1.021, 1.020) sit in a plausible window and exceed the leads, which is weak support for a lagged signal. The 4-week dip (0.989) has no mechanistic counterpart in the notes and is an unexplained feature of the data.

**The intervals allow for overdispersion but not for correlation.** Every regression declares overdispersed errors, scaling the standard errors by Pearson chi-square over residual degrees of freedom (quasi-Poisson). Check 37 matches statsmodels `scale='X2'` to within 1e-5, so dispersion of about 5 is already reflected in the reported intervals. What the intervals do not allow for is correlation: between successive weeks within a municipality (which composite sharing reinforces), and between municipalities that share weather, cloud regimes or, for `tempmed` in the station era, the same airport station. No HAC or cluster-robust errors are used. If such correlation is present, the reported intervals are narrower than the data support. Its size is not established.

The log-linear shape is a conditional approximation. Over a range sitting below the 29 °C optimum, a constant per-degree rate approximates the rising limb of the response. But the 29 °C optimum is an air-temperature figure, and daytime LST cannot be placed on that axis without a calibration that does not exist for Rio de Janeiro. Rio de Janeiro state spans cool mountain municipalities near the uncertain cold limit and warm lowlands approaching the optimum, and a single pooled slope averages the two. Field studies in Brazil and the Caribbean consistently found non-linear lagged associations. In Barbados, Tmin was "a better predictor than mean and maximum temperature", and bidimensional exposure-lag functions outperformed linear single-lag terms ([Lowe et al. 2018, Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC6049902)). In Minas Gerais the rise flattened at extreme heat (RR 1.10, 0.99–1.22), with I² of 60% ([Gomes et al. 2024, Cad Saúde Pública, Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC10962433)). None of these used daytime LST. No Brazilian field study found here demonstrates a turnover at 29 °C, and none compares exposures (daytime LST, night LST, reanalysis or station) for dengue in the same population.

The weekly rainfall adjustment carries two burdens. The first is ordinary confounding: rainy weeks are cooler and cloudier, and for LST also wetter at the surface. A linear CHIRPS term is a coarse control for this. The second is the drought pathway, which works at 3–5 months. The month strata mostly absorb it, so it threatens the estimate only through hot, dry weeks within a month. Those are exactly the weeks clear-sky LST retains and reads hottest; this is an inference, untested here. The CHIRPS term cannot represent filling, flushing or storage, and the rain it counts misses the tanks and drums that dominate pupal production where supply is intermittent.

The following options are presented with their evidence; none is a settled correction.

## Evidence ranked from established to absent

The firmest conclusions are mechanistic. A unimodal temperature response with a sharp hot limit rests on multiple open laboratory syntheses (Mordecai 2017, 2019; Brady 2013). That fluctuation changes transmission, with a sign set by the mean, rests on consistent experiments and models, though each used only one or two colonies (Lambrechts 2011; Carrington 2013). Five points have moderate support. Drought raises Brazilian dengue at 3–5 months, especially in urban areas, resting on one national full-text study plus the same pattern in other settings from a shared team and method. Large containers that people fill dominate pupal production in the Brazilian cities surveyed (Goiânia abstract; Fortaleza). Daytime LST runs warm of air and agrees worse than night LST, consistently across validation studies, none of them in a Brazilian city. CHIRPS is adequate at monthly scale in two RJ municipalities but biased by up to 52% at a coastal gauge. Reanalysis means are accurate to within about 1 °C in other Brazilian regions.

Weak support, from one or two studies outside Brazil or from abstracts only, covers the rest: resting and indoor sites share the outdoor mean but narrow the daily range; station error varies with season and land cover; container water departs from air by material; low humidity shortens adult life in a way applicable to a humid coastal state; and *Aedes* abundance follows night LST more closely than day LST.

**Not established by any source here:** LST, station or reanalysis offsets at resting sites in any Brazilian city; any calibration of MODIS LST against air temperature in a Brazilian city; how faithfully within-month LST or `tempmed` anomalies track air temperature in Rio de Janeiro state; whether dengue's temperature relation in clear-sky weeks matches that in cloudy weeks; the date of InfoDengue's source switch or any reprocessing; and weekly CHIRPS skill in the state. No study has measured how exposure choice changes a dengue exposure-response curve in one population. There is no measured pathway from drought through storage and mosquitoes to cases, no direct effect of humidity on vector competence, and no turnover at 29 °C in Brazilian field data. Several studies were read only as abstracts or snippets (Kumar 2018, Pilger 2011, Lowe 2018, Gomes 2024, Liu-Helmersson 2014, Araújo 2022, Moreno-Madriñán 2014 and most of the LST validation literature). Their numbers should be checked against the full texts before anyone relies on them.

## Conclusion

The literature recasts the fitted coefficients as statements about a surface, not about a mosquito. With municipality × year × month strata, the W5 model asks whether a sunny week's roof-and-road temperature, within a month, predicts dengue weeks later, among the weeks that clouds allowed. Mean offsets drop out under that design. What remains depends on how much of the within-month LST signal is air temperature and how much is sky and surface wetness, which nobody has measured in Rio de Janeiro. The biology adds that the daytime peak is the part of the thermal exposure indoor resting removes, and the field studies point to minimum temperature instead. The lag-0 coefficient is indistinguishable from the leads, partly by the composite's construction. Whatever temperature signal the model holds sits in the 6–8 week coefficients. Testing even that calls for correlation-aware errors and a comparison against night LST or the air-temperature columns the file already carries.

The most decision-relevant gap could be closed directly. A short logger campaign in a few Rio de Janeiro municipalities would give the first Brazilian numbers on what *Ae. aegypti* actually meets. It would pair indoor resting-site, outdoor shade and stored-water temperatures with the matching MODIS day and night pixels, the municipal ERA5-Land series and the old airport station. Until then, daytime LST is a measure of exposed urban surfaces, and its per-degree rate ratios do not translate into degrees of air. `tempmed` and `tempmin` are closer stand-ins for the mean and night-time thermal environment, and nothing more. `precip_total_semana` measures rain on the city, not water in the containers.
